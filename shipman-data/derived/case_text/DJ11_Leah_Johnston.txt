Leah Johnston
Introduction
Mrs Leah Johnston died at Charnley House, a residential home for the elderly, Albert Road, Hyde on Sunday, 11th
November 1979 at the age of 80. It is unclear whether she was registered as a patient of Shipman. If she was, she had been a patient of his for less than a week when she died. He certified that the cause of her death was coronary thrombosis.
In September 2001, the Inquiry published a list of deaths, the cause of which had been certified by Shipman, where the Inquiry had been unable to trace a relative of the deceased. In response to that advertisement, Mr Vincent Johnston (Mrs Johnston's son) and Mrs Kathleen Lee (Mrs Johnston's granddaughter) contacted the Inquiry and provided statements of evidence. The Inquiry has also taken statements from Mrs Ann Johnston (Mr Johnston's wife), Mr Sidney Pickford (Mrs Johnston's son-in-law), Mrs Lilian Stansfield (the former matron and manager of Charnley House) and Mrs Helen Spencer (in 1979, a trainee nurse working as a care assistant at Charnley House). Mrs Mary Bloxham, Mrs Johnston's daughter, has told the Inquiry that she can add no further useful information to that already available.
The Inquiry has also had access to the copy entry in the register of deaths ('death certificate'), the Medical Certificate of Cause of Death (MCCD) counterfoil, the Charnley House daily diary and Shipman's visits book for 1979. Mrs Johnston's medical records have not survived.
This decision has been written following consideration of the papers and without an oral hearing.
Personal Background
Mrs Johnston was a widow with eight children of whom only Mr Johnston and his sister Mrs Bloxham survive.
Mrs Johnston suffered from arthritis, bronchitis and asthma for many years before her death. Mr Johnston recalls that each day she used to take a white tablet, possibly ephedrine, for the relief of her asthma. Ephedrine is a decongestant. Mr Johnston does not think that his mother suffered from any heart problems. He recalls that she told him that a doctor had told her that she had a very strong heart. Since Mrs Lee remembers that her grandmother also had tablets that she used to place under her tongue to help with her breathing, it seems likely that she had angina due to ischaemic heart disease.
Prior to moving to Charnley House, Mrs Johnston had lived at St George's House, warden-controlled accommodation in Stalybridge. There, according to Mr Johnston, she lived a life of complete independence and did not require assistance with daily chores or the management of her affairs. Then, two years before she died, Mrs Johnston began to suffer from paranoia. She was treated for a few weeks at Hyde Hospital from where she was discharged to Charnley House only six days before she died. She did not settle at Charnley House and seemed depressed, in part on account of her lack of independence. She also continued to suffer episodes of paranoia. Although most of the rooms at Charnley House were shared, Mrs Johnston had her own room.
Mr Johnston visited his mother on two or three occasions at Charnley House. She was also visited by her daughters. In the weeks leading up to his mother's death, Mr Johnston did not notice any deterioration in her condition, although Mr Pickford thought that she was 'off colour' during the last week of her life. According to Mr Pickford, he and his wife Charlotte used to visit each afternoon.
The Days Leading up to the Death
The daily diary records that, on the morning of Friday, 9th
November 1979, Mrs Johnston refused to go to
'day-care'
. My understanding is that some of the Charnley House residents went to a day-care centre. Although Mrs Spencer did not herself make that entry, she says that it suggests to her that Mrs Johnston was physically well enough to go to day-care but decided for some reason that she did not wish to go.
The daily diary records that, on the morning of Saturday, 10th
November, Mrs Johnston was eating normally.
Mr Pickford says that he and his wife visited Mrs Johnston during the afternoon of Saturday, 10th
November. He remembers spending two to three hours with her and leaving at around 7pm or 8pm. He has described sitting and talking with Mrs Johnston in the lounge during that time. He has said that she was 'reasonably well' when they left that evening, although she was complaining about her breathing. In view of the entries in the daily diary, to which I am about to refer, it seems likely that Mr Pickford is mistaken, either as to the time of his visit on the Saturday afternoon or, possibly, as to the date of his last visit. As he says that he and his wife visited Mrs Johnston every afternoon, it is perhaps likely that their Saturday visit was earlier and shorter than he recalls. As these events took place many years ago, this kind of error would be wholly understandable.

suggests that an injection was not administered on that occasion. She believes that, if an injection had been given, she would have recorded the fact in the report.
It remains unclear whether the doctor who visited Mrs Johnston at 4.55pm on Saturday, 10th
November was Shipman or some other doctor. It is recorded within Shipman's visits book for that day that he was the doctor
'on duty'
, presumably under the out of hours rota operated by the doctors at the Donneybrook Surgery. It is recorded in his visits book that he was to visit Mrs Stocks, another patient at Charnley House, that day. The corresponding entry in the daily diary records that Shipman did indeed visit Mrs Stocks, but that note appears in the morning report. As Shipman was on out of hours duty, he would have been called out in the late afternoon, unless he had diverted calls through to the deputising service. The fact that the doctor who attended at 4.55pm is not identified by name might suggest that a deputising doctor came, because Shipman is referred to by name on other days within the daily diary. However, the entries identifying Shipman by name are in handwriting other than that of Mrs Spencer, and it seems likely to me that it was Shipman who attended in the late afternoon but for some reason Mrs Spencer did not identify him by name. I am fortified in that view because that doctor promised to visit Mrs Johnston the following day and it was Shipman who came the next day.

Mr Johnston says that he was not informed on 10th
November that his mother was unwell or that she had been diagnosed as suffering from a heart attack. It does not seem that he attended the home either and the likelihood is that, if contact was made at all, it was with other members of the family.
Mrs Lee, who at the time of her grandmother's death was 15 years of age, remembers being told by her mother that Shipman had visited Mrs Johnston 'a couple of days before she died' and said that she had a thrombosis in her leg. Mrs Johnston had apparently believed that this was life-threatening and that she was dying. When the doctor had left, Mrs Johnston apparently called for a priest because she 'needed the last rites'. If that account were correct, it would suggest that the doctor who attended at 4.55pm was probably Shipman and the account is a description of that consultation. Having said that, Mrs Lee's recollection of events may be inaccurate. She was very young at the time. It is unlikely that she would have remembered the name of the doctor with whom her grandmother had consulted more than 20 years before.
The Day of the Death
The morning report in the daily diary records the following entry in respect of Mrs Johnston:
'Seen by Dr. Shipman (injection given) is now sleeping.
Dr. Shipman will visit again this evening around 6pm'
.
Mrs Stansfield says that she cannot remember what medication would have been administered by injection, although she does recall that doctors who visited the home would 'administer a drop of morphine to residents if they were in pain'. She speculates that an antibiotic might have been given if Mrs Johnston was suffering from an infection that caused her to have a high temperature, although there is in fact no evidence that Mrs Johnston had a high temperature. She speculates, alternatively, that the injection might have been related to her coronary thrombosis.
Although the timing of Shipman's visit on 11th
November is unclear, some assistance is provided by Mrs Stansfield's evidence that her shift finished at 1pm on 11th
November, but that she would not leave Charnley House until closer to 2pm. I infer that Shipman must have visited and given the injection and that Mrs Johnston must have been sleeping by the time Mrs Stansfield wrote her shift report, some time before 2pm.
The final entry relating to Mrs Johnston appears in the afternoon report, and records as follows:
'Passed away peacefully at 2PM
Dr Shipman certified her dead, undertaker
informed, body taken at 4.15PM.
Relatives have taken handbag and spectacules
(
sic
)
'
.
There are no names in Shipman's visits book for Sunday, 11th
November. Shipman has written only the words
'on duty'
.
Mrs Ann Johnston recalls that she and her husband received a telephone call from Charnley House on the morning of 11th
November to say that Mrs Johnston was unwell and they decided to visit. Mr Johnston does not remember that call, although he does remember going to Charnley House that day. In a letter to the Inquiry, dated 10th
September 2001, Mr Johnston said that he remembered that his journey to Charnley House was delayed by the Remembrance Day parades. He specifically remembered that, as the clock at Hyde Town Hall struck 11am, he was at the traffic lights on Market Street. In his witness statement, however, he says that he and his wife arrived at Charnley House between 10am and 11am. He remembers that the front door was locked and that they were initially unable to get into the house. After some minutes, the front door was opened and he went in to see his mother. There was a woman sitting on the end of his mother's bed, whom he did not recognise, who turned to him and said, 'Your mother has just passed away. She has had a heart attack.' He remembers that his mother was lying in the bed, under the covers, wearing her nightclothes. He has described beads of sweat on her face, and said that her mouth was open, 'almost as if she was trying to catch a last breath of air'. He also noticed that her lips were quite pale and colourless. Mrs Ann Johnston agrees with her husband's statement.
Mr Pickford says that he did not see Mrs Johnston on the day of her death until after she had died. He says that, when he reached Charnley House at some time in the afternoon, he was told that his mother-in-law had died shortly before his arrival. He saw his mother-in-law on the bed, dressed in her ordinary day clothes. He does not think that she was wearing nightclothes.
Certification
Shipman must have completed the MCCD. Although the certificate itself has not survived, its counterfoil has been found. On that, Shipman stated that Mrs Johnston had died of a coronary thrombosis on 11th
November. He said that he had last seen her alive on the day of the death. He had seen the body after death. There would be no post-mortem examination. The MCCD counterfoil is dated 11th
November 1979.
Conclusion
It seems likely that Mrs Johnston had a history of heart disease and that she suffered a heart attack in the late afternoon of 10th
November 1979. The description of her condition, as recorded in the daily diary, shows that she was cold, clammy and in pain. She was sweating profusely and had a very slow and irregular pulse. Shipman, for I believe it was he who attended, diagnosed a heart attack. It is not known what medication he prescribed. If Mrs Johnston were still in pain when he attended, it would have been in order for him to give a small injection of morphine or diamorphine to relieve the pain. There is no evidence that he did so on that occasion. It may be that the pain had subsided by the time he arrived. Having suffered this heart attack, Mrs Johnston would certainly have been at risk of suffering a further episode.
There is conflicting evidence as to the time of Mrs Johnston's death on 11th
November. Mr Johnston's recollection of the Town Hall clock striking 11 o'clock is certainly vivid, but he did not repeat that account in his statement. The daily diary records the time of death as 2pm. I think it is likely that the death took place at about 2pm. In my experience, the staff at Charnley House were usually careful to note the time of a death. If the death had occurred at about 11am, it would have been recorded by Mrs Stansfield in the morning report. Mr Pickford recalls that the death occurred at some time in the afternoon.
There is no evidence of the time at which Shipman visited on 11th
November and gave an injection. We do not know in what condition Mrs Johnston was when he arrived. There is no note in the daily diary to show that she was in pain or to suggest that she had suffered another heart attack. She could have done but it may be that she was just unwell as the result of the previous day's attack. Nor do we know what drug Shipman injected. Mrs Stansfield suggests that the doctors would sometimes give 'a drop of morphine' to patients who were in pain or distress but says she does not know what Shipman gave Mrs Johnston.
It appears that, at some time after the injection was given, Mrs Johnston fell asleep and never awoke. It is certainly possible that she died within an hour of receiving the injection. It is possible that she died rather more quickly than that. I note that the diary does not say that Shipman was sent for after the death and it appears to me quite possible that he had not left the building by the time the death had occurred. It is possible, therefore, that Mrs Johnston died shortly after Shipman had given her an injection.
This case gives rise to some suspicion that Shipman might have killed Mrs Johnston. However, I am unable to reach a decision, even on the balance of probabilities, about the circumstances of this death. There are too many uncertainties. It is possible that Shipman gave an excessive dose of opiate to Mrs Johnston and killed her. On the other hand, it is quite possible that he gave an appropriate dose of something, such as an opiate or a sedative, and that Mrs Johnston died of natural causes. There is a further possibility, which is that he gave a dose of opiate or sedative which was not sufficient to kill Mrs Johnston, but was larger than was appropriate for therapeutic purposes and was intended to ensure that she did not wake up again before her death.
