Muriel Margaret Ward
Introduction
Mrs Muriel Margaret Ward (known as Molly) died at her home, 41 Ogden Court, Frank Street, Hyde on Tuesday, 24th
October 1995 at the age of 87. She had been a patient of Shipman since 1990. He certified that the cause of her death was coronary thrombosis due to ischaemic heart disease.
On 14th
October 1998, Mrs Ward's death came to the attention of the police when they interviewed the warden at Ogden Court, Mrs Sandra Christine Simpson, in connection with the death of Mrs Ward's daughter, Miss Maureen Ward, of whose murder Shipman was later convicted. Mrs Simpson raised concerns about a number of other deaths in which she suspected that Shipman might have been involved. The police investigated those deaths and, in relation to Mrs Ward's case, obtained statements from Mrs Simpson, from Mrs Hannah Marsh and Mr Alan Johnson (two of Mrs Ward's friends), and from Ms Margaret Anne Booth (a paramedic). The police also took a statement from Dr John Grenville.
Shipman was not prosecuted for causing the death of Mrs Ward but an inquest into her death was opened and adjourned by the South Manchester Coroner on 18th
May 2001.
The Inquiry has taken a further statement from Mrs Simpson and a statement from Mrs Christine Knowles, who was the duty warden on call when Mrs Ward's death was discovered. The Inquiry has also had access to Mrs Ward's general practitioner and hospital records and to appointments sheets, visits books and other practice documents for the relevant period. The Inquiry also obtained the diary from Ogden Court and relevant documents from the ambulance service.
This decision has been made following consideration of the papers and without an oral hearing.
Personal Background
Mrs Ward had lived at Ogden Court for many years and her daughter, Miss Ward, returned to live with her in about February 1995.
Mrs Ward's medical records reveal that in, 1984, she was found to have a heart problem, described as intermittent supraventricular tachycardia. After treatment with various different types of medication, her condition stabilised, although she continued to suffer palpitations. The cause of her tachycardia was never determined but, according to correspondence from the hospital, her consultants felt that it might be due to ischaemic heart disease. Dr Grenville thinks that Mrs Ward might also have had a mild degree of heart failure when her supraventricular tachycardia was first discovered. She was treated for this for the rest of her life. There are no further signs or symptoms of heart failure recorded in her notes.
In August 1995, Mrs Ward had a fall and fractured her hip. She was admitted to hospital and made an excellent recovery although, while she was convalescing, she developed a deep vein thrombosis. She was put on anticoagulant treatment with warfarin and was discharged home on 8th
September 1995. The clotting factor in her blood was to be monitored thereafter and Shipman visited weekly to take a blood sample.
The Day of the Death
On the day of Mrs Ward's death, Mrs Simpson worked a split shift, from 8.30am to 1.30pm and then from 4.30pm to 7.30pm. She had formed the opinion that Mrs Ward's post-operative convalescence was going well, although her mobility was still impaired. She saw Mrs Ward on her morning rounds. She cannot now recall Mrs Ward's state of health but there is no note relating to Mrs Ward in her diary for that day, from which she infers that Mrs Ward was well.
The surgery visits book contains the following entry for 24th
October 1995:
'
MURIEL WARD - REVISIT'
.
It appears that Mrs Ward's friend, Mrs Marsh, arrived to visit Mrs Ward while Shipman was in the flat. She pressed the buzzer and a male, who must have been Shipman, asked her to come back in half an hour. He said he was with Mrs Ward. Mrs Marsh went to sit with Mrs Ward's neighbour at Number 39, and it was only a few minutes later that she was informed that Mrs Ward had died.
The evidence of the circumstances of Shipman's visit comes from Mr Johnson, who received an account from Miss Ward about a month later. Miss Ward told Mr Johnson that the doctor had arrived to see her mother just as she herself was about to go out to the shops. She let the doctor in to see her mother and went shopping. When she returned about half an hour later, she found her mother dead. She was terribly shocked.
It appears that Miss Ward must then have pulled the alarm cord to summon the warden or telephoned for Shipman or possibly both. Mrs Simpson was off duty and the alarm call was put through to Eldercare, who provided a mobile warden service while Mrs Simpson was not on duty. That call was received at 2.37pm and the note says that Miss Ward thought her mother was dead in her chair. Eldercare summoned an ambulance and it is possible they also summoned Shipman. In any event, it appears that Shipman must have attended very quickly. Miss Ward later told Mr Johnson that Shipman told her that Mrs Ward had been 'Okay' when he left at the end of his earlier visit.
Shipman was just leaving when Mrs Knowles, the warden, arrived at Ogden Court. Mrs Knowles introduced herself. Shipman was abrupt and dismissive. He said that Mrs Ward was dead, got into his car and drove off. Mrs Knowles went into the flat and met Miss Ward. She saw the body of Mrs Ward sitting in a chair in the living room. Her face was turned towards the window as if she were looking outside. Her eyes were still open. She had a very peaceful look on her face and appeared as though she was deep in thought. A few minutes later, Miss Ward must have gone to Mrs Ward's neighbour and broken the news to Mrs Marsh. Mrs Marsh was very shocked to hear of the death as she had thought that her friend was 'on the mend' after her fall.
Mrs Knowles made the following entry in the Ogden Court diary:
'Called out to F41. Mrs Ward was found by her daughter, she had passed away. The doctor was there when I arrived'
.
Mrs Simpson told the police that, upon her return to work at 4.30pm, she learned that Mrs Knowles had been called to Mrs Ward's flat because her alarm cord had been activated. She went to Flat 41 but, by that stage, Mrs Knowles had left. Mrs Ward's body had also been removed. However, Miss Ward was there and she told Mrs Simpson that she had been out to do some shopping at Kwik Save and had found her mother dead when she returned. She said she had only been out for a few minutes.
The ambulance service record shows that the ambulance was present at 14.43 hours. The paramedics found no vital signs present. No electrocardiograph (ECG) trace was taken and the recorded reason for this was:
'No trace taken. gp had only left at 14.00
daughter was distressed when we arrived'
.

The question must arise as to whether that note was genuine or was fabricated as part of Shipman's concealment of his killing of Mrs Ward. It could be either.

Certification
Shipman completed the Medical Certificate of Cause of Death (MCCD). He certified the cause of death to be coronary thrombosis, due to ischaemic heart disease. He said that only
'minutes'
elapsed between the onset of the coronary thrombosis and death. Ischaemic heart disease had been present for 11 years. He also said that congestive heart failure of five years' duration had contributed to the death, although this was not related to the coronary thrombosis. He said that he had last seen Mrs Ward alive on the day of the death.
Shipman completed Form B of the cremation certificate. Shipman wrote that death had occurred at
'14.30'
on 24th
October 1995, although he appears originally to have written 12.30 (or, possibly, 2.30). He stated that he attended Mrs Ward during her last illness for a period of three months. If that last illness was ischaemic heart disease, this was a condition from which she had suffered for 11 years, according to the MCCD. If Shipman was referring to the fractured hip, which had occurred in August, it would not be correct to describe that as a last illness, as it did not cause her death. The medical notes do not show any deterioration in Mrs Ward's ischaemic heart disease during the last three months of her life, or indeed any reference to it at all during the last two years.
Shipman stated that he last saw Mrs Ward alive about 15 minutes before her death. He saw the body about 20 minutes after death and made a complete external examination of it. He certified the causes as on the MCCD. He stated that the mode of death was collapse of
'seconds only'
duration. His opinion as to the mode and cause of death was based on his own observations and statements made by Mrs Ward's daughter. He said that no one was present at the time of death.
Shipman noted that Mrs Ward had undergone the operative repair of her fractured femur within the last year. In response to the question as to whether the coroner had been notified, Shipman wrote:
'Checked with coroner. He was agreeable to a D.C. being signed. No action needed'
.
The Inquiry has asked the coroner's office whether they have any record of such contact. In a letter dated 8th
August 2001, the coroner's officer, Mrs Blake, informed the Inquiry that she could find no reference in the coroner's records to Mrs Ward's death or to the fact that Shipman had contacted the coroner after her death. The Inquiry asked Mrs Blake whether she would expect to see such a record. In a letter dated 14th
September 2001, she told the Inquiry:
'If Shipman had rang
(
sic
)
the office and reported the death to us then obviously we would have a record of it, in this case he did not report the death to us either for a PM or to say that he would be happy to issue a certificate if Coroner agreed i.e. if he was perhaps out of the 14 day deadline or the patient had been terminally ill and had been looked after by Macmillan Nurses etc. the patient does not always see a doctor on a regular basis, in either case we would have issued an 'A' side to cover Shipman's certificate.
If Shipman just rang to discuss and seek some advice about the case which sometimes happens with many doctors and the Coroner agreed he could issue, a record would not have been made. This is not the case since computerisation as all reports are now logged. In this case I do not know why he would have a need to ring the office at all especially as...he had seen the lady 15 minutes prior to her death'
.
It appears likely that Shipman contacted the coroner's office to mention the recent surgery, which he would probably have said had had no bearing on the death; that would explain why the coroner or a member of his staff indicated that Shipman could sign the MCCD.
The Expert Evidence
In his police statement, Dr Grenville said:
'Assuming that Shipman visited between 2pm and 2.30pm

and found Mrs Ward as he has described her in his hand written note and assuming that he was called back less than one hour later because she was dead it would not have been possible for him to sign a certificate of the cause of death. His record gives no indication of anything which might lead to sudden death and no one was present when Mrs Ward died. Shipman should have informed the Coroner of Mrs Ward's sudden and unexpected death with a view to a post mortem examination being performed. Even if...Shipman felt that Mrs Ward had died of natural causes and wished to complete a certificate of the cause of death there are two other causes of sudden and unexpected death which are more likely than the coronary thrombosis which Shipman has certified. These are a fatal cardiac arrhymia

Professor Richard Baker found the death highly suspicious on the basis of the information contained both in the medical records and in the cremation documentation.
Conclusion
Mrs Ward had some history of heart disease and was probably at an increased risk of heart attack. However, the circumstances of her death make it extremely unlikely that this was the cause of her death.
The most obviously suspicious feature is that the death was discovered within a very short time of Shipman's first visit to Mrs Ward. Working back from the time at which Miss Ward alerted Eldercare (2.37pm), it would appear likely that she arrived back from the shops at about 2.30 to 2.35pm. She had only been to do some shopping at Kwik Save and she thought she had not been out for more than a few minutes according to Mrs Simpson. More likely, she would have been out for about half an hour. On Form B, Shipman suggested that the death occurred at 2.30pm and that he had been with Mrs Ward 15 minutes earlier. It appears to me that Shipman probably arrived at or shortly after 2pm. Thus, Mrs Ward was alive and well when Shipman arrived and Miss Ward left to go shopping. According to Shipman, Mrs Ward was well when he left at 2.15pm. Fifteen minutes later, she was dead. It is of course physically possible to suffer a fatal heart attack within that short time span. If the doctor involved were not Shipman, one would say that it was strange and remarkable that Mrs Ward should die just after the doctor had left, but one would not say it was wholly unbelievable. However, with Shipman, a known serial killer, such a coincidence is beyond belief. That evidence alone persuades me that Shipman must have killed Mrs Ward. However, there are other aspects of the evidence that point to the same conclusion.
If events had taken place as Shipman suggested, he would not have had any real idea of why Mrs Ward had died, shortly after his departure. As Dr Grenville said, Mrs Ward was certainly as much at risk of dying of cardiac arrhythmia or pulmonary embolism as coronary thrombosis. No one had seen Mrs Ward in her death throes so as to provide Shipman with information from which he could infer that she had had a heart attack. This death should have been referred to the coroner. However, Shipman would not wish that to happen as, if the post-mortem examination had not revealed a plausible cause of death, the coroner might have commissioned toxicological tests.
Mrs Ward was found sitting in her chair, looking peaceful. Her appearance in death was not consistent with the appearance of one who had died of a coronary thrombosis. It was, however, entirely typical of many of Shipman's victims who had been killed by lethal injection of diamorphine.
I am sure that Shipman killed Mrs Ward, as he was later to kill her daughter, almost certainly by a lethal injection of diamorphine.
